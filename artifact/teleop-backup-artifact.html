Reproducible benchmark · open-source

Can Bluetooth be a robot’s backup safety link?

Teleoperated robots need a kill‑switch that keeps working even when the main wireless link drops. We tested whether ordinary Bluetooth — the kind in every phone and laptop — can be that independent backup. It can. And the result that matters: we found the one way it silently fails — a stop signal that stays fast until you overload the link, then jumps ~20× — and reduced it to a simple design rule. So that failure mode shows up here, in a benchmark, instead of on a live robot.

In one minute

A backup safety channel has to do two tiny things reliably: send a heartbeat (“I’m still here”) and carry a stop signal the instant something goes wrong. Bluetooth handles both easily — when the link is quiet.

The catch is what happens when you also push bulk data over the same link (status, telemetry, a camera buffer). Below a certain load everything stays fast. Push the link to its limit and the stop signal suddenly takes 20× longer to get through — long enough to matter for safety.

The rule that makes it safe

Never run the safety link at 100%. Keep bulk data below about three‑quarters of capacity or give the stop signal its own dedicated lane. Do either, and the stop signal stays fast under any load.

The finding — a cliff, not a slope

We measured how long a stop signal takes to make a round trip while bulk data runs alongside it, as we dialed the bulk load up. The response time is flat and fast — until the link saturates, where it falls off a cliff.

The fix — two ways, both work

Under a fully saturated link, the stop signal’s delay depends entirely on how you arrange the traffic. Sharing one lane is catastrophic; either fix brings it back under the safety budget.

The dead-man’s-switch

A safety link is only as good as what happens when it goes quiet. Here, a lost heartbeat is carried through to a physical STOP pin — a real dead-man’s-switch, bench-timed on hardware, not a software promise.

~200 ms
from last heartbeat to the STOP pin asserting — well inside a 300–700 ms stop budget
fail-safe

Free software vs. proprietary

There are two ways to run the Bluetooth radio: the open-source controller (free, inspectable line-by-line — we patched in the Bluetooth 6.0 speed feature ourselves), or the chip vendor’s proprietary controller (licensed, closed, speed feature built in). For a safety link you can audit, the open stack is the appealing choice — so the only question is whether it costs you performance. It doesn’t.

For a robotics fleet that means no vendor lock-in for the safety link: the open, auditable controller performs like the proprietary one, and you keep the ability to inspect and reproduce every byte — which for a safety-critical channel is a feature, not a footnote.

What’s new here

We ran a documented search of the published literature, patents, and vendor material. The core result appears to be unmeasured anywhere.

  • NovelThe latency cliff itself — a stop signal on this kind of Bluetooth link going from ~10 ms to ~290 ms exactly at the point of saturation. Nobody has published this measurement. The nearest reference is a 2005 patent that asserts the problem but never measured it.
  • NovelThe measured comparison of the two fixes — that pacing reliably holds the stop signal under budget, and that a dedicated lane is a promising alternative for un-paceable bulk (single-session so far), with real numbers for each.
  • NovelThe first public measurement of Bluetooth 6.0’s new speed feature (“Frame Space Update”). We found no published throughput figure for it anywhere — academic, vendor, or standards. We measured a +9–16% gain and, more usefully, pinned down when it helps: only when the link is already packed to capacity. On a lightly loaded safety link it does nothing — a distinction no one had characterised. We also physically confirmed it on air: an independent radio observer watched the on-air inter-packet timing shrink 150 → 52 µs — proof the feature is really running, not just negotiated.
  • NovelA reproducible, open-source benchmark on freely available hardware — anyone can verify it, rather than trust a datasheet.

Why it matters for teleoperation

The expensive, long-range radios that carry teleop video and control are excellent — but their redundancy is homogeneous: same vendor, same silicon, same power domain. A single firmware fault or a jammer on their band can take every fallback path down at once.

A Bluetooth safety link is heterogeneous backup — different chip, different radio, different power, different frequency. It can’t carry video, and it isn’t meant to. Its job is the one thing that must never drop: the heartbeat and the stop. When the primary link fails, an independent channel on unrelated hardware is what keeps a robot from becoming a runaway.

The takeaway for a fleet

Bluetooth is a viable, cheap, independent safety fallback. Design it to a simple rule — don’t saturate the link, and put the stop signal on its own lane — and the kill‑switch stays fast under any load, on hardware that fails independently of your primary radio.

The honest gaps

What we have not yet proven, stated plainly — a safety claim with no caveats is itself a red flag:

  • OpenPoor radio conditions. Most testing was on a clean bench; behaviour under interference and at range is the key open gate before any safety sign-off. (This fleet runs Wi‑Fi on 5 GHz and BLE on 2.4 GHz, which largely keeps the safety link out of its own robot’s Wi‑Fi band — though same‑device front‑end effects and a crowded 2.4 GHz floor still need measuring.)
  • OpenThe production stop circuit. The timing is proven; the real actuator wiring and a dedicated safety-watchdog part are not yet built.
  • OpenOne reconnect path. A relay power-cycle can stall the high-rate bulk stream until it re-establishes — safety detection is unaffected, but it is a known rough edge.
  • ScopeNot a certification. This is a suitability map for an engineering decision, not a device qualification or safety rating — a safety owner still sets the approved timing budget.

How we know

Built to be checked, not trusted. Every number is measured on two off-the-shelf nRF54L15 boards (~$100 total) at a fixed operating point, each confirmed by re-test — and the firmware (prebuilt and buildable), the step-by-step guide, the measurement instrumentation, and the raw logs are all open source, so anyone can reproduce it rather than trust a datasheet. On provenance: the baseline numbers run on unmodified, released Bluetooth software (Zephyr); the Bluetooth 6.0 speed-feature numbers run on our own controller build of that feature — which isn’t in any released open version yet (only the proprietary controller ships it), so on the open stack we are running, and have measured on air, a capability the ecosystem hasn’t shipped. Absolute speeds depend on your radio environment, so the transferable results are the shapes — the cliff, and the fixes. This page reflects the latest run and supersedes earlier drafts.
