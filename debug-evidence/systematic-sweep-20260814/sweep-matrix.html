Open-source Bluetooth for a teleop backup link: the throughput and latency, measured

If Wi-Fi drops, can a Bluetooth radio carry the backup essentials — heartbeat, stop-signal, telemetry? Measured end-to-end on real hardware, here's how fast and how low-latency it is. This characterizes the link — it's not a full safety qualification; the FAQ below marks exactly what's still open.

Round-trip latency

~27–48 ms idle, at the useful intervals — ~1.6–2.0× the link interval

At 15–25 ms intervals, an idle command-and-acknowledge round-trip is ~27–48 ms. Add concurrent telemetry and the tail grows — but simple send-pacing keeps it in check (see "latency under load" below). Whether that fits your emergency-stop budget is yours to confirm.

FOSS vs proprietary

Comparable overall — but neither stack dominates

Open-source Zephyr and Nordic's proprietary SoftDevice trade the lead across the operating space — each ahead in different regimes, gaps of 15–25% in places, converging elsewhere. No consistent winner on throughput or latency, so the open stack (no license fee, no vendor lock-in, auditable) is a real option, not a performance sacrifice.

How fast

Peaks at ~170–185 KB/s — control + telemetry, not video

Peaks at ~170–185 KB/s, around 15–25 ms intervals — ample for a heartbeat, a stop-signal, and concurrent telemetry, but a control-and-telemetry link, not a video pipe.

The detailed data — for your firmware engineer

Throughput across the whole operating space

What's new in this study — the results we could not find published anywhere (established BLE behavior we merely re-measured is catalogued as prior art at the end of this section):

  • A working, on-air-verified FSU on the open controller — and its measured throughput dividend. The open-stack FSU controller code was unmerged upstream WIP (see prior art below); we finished it into something that runs and negotiates on air, then measured the gain. Bluetooth 6.0's Frame Space Update is documented qualitatively everywhere, but we found no public source giving a measured %-gain vs. interval — the solid–dashed gaps below are, to our knowledge, the first.
  • Latency under concurrent load. Prior benchmarks measure latency or throughput; the stop-signal RTT tail while bulk saturates the same link — and the completion-pacing fix (queue depth, not throughput, sets the tail) — is the new, safety-relevant result.
  • Same-session open-vs-proprietary head-to-head across the whole interval × transport space, rather than one operating point.

"No published figure found" = a documented web/literature search this study, not an exhaustive survey — treat as novel to our knowledge, not a priority claim. A separate ~915 µs open-stack round-trip result (not on this page) is likewise novel-to-our-knowledge vs. the closest published figures (Ezurio ~859 µs proprietary/one-way; ZMK ~2.8 ms open).

How to read the panels. Each is one data path — GATT (the usual characteristic-based path) or the L2CAP CoC byte pipe — sent one-way (bulk one direction, only ACKs back — a measurement choice, not a device limit) or both directions at once (duplex; shares the half-duplex air, so the total doesn't just double). Solid vs. dashed = with/without Bluetooth 6.0's FSU (a shorter inter-packet gap); the shaded gap is what FSU adds.

Data note: 84 cells, one bench session, but a composite — the open-CoC 50/75/100 ms points are later deep-buffer re-runs (n=2), and the latency panel is a separate pass. Most cells are single runs, so read a solid–dashed gap as an observed difference, not an effect with error bars.

Reading the shapes. Several paths peak or plateau around 15–25 ms; open GATT one-way is highest at 7.5 ms. Throughput falls past ~50 ms — fewer events per second stop being repaid by fuller events. The FSU on/off difference is largest on GATT one-way, but it is not zero on GATT duplex either — it grows with interval there (e.g. open +15% at 37.5 ms, SDC +17% at 50 ms); a few single-run cells even show FSU-on below FSU-off. Open's CoC climbs to ~170 at 15 ms while proprietary SDC's CoC stays flat ~143 — but that lead reverses at long intervals (SDC ~143 vs open ~120 at 100 ms). The two stacks trade the lead by regime rather than one matching the other.

Latency — what the interval costs

Latency tracks the interval, not the stack. Idle round-trip ≈ 1.6–2.0× the connection interval, and open and SDC land on the same line — no controller difference is detectable in these idle measurements. So the throughput sweet spot (15–25 ms) costs ~27–48 ms idle round-trip. Shown to 37.5 ms; beyond that the rig's ping-timeout truncates the measurement (those points aren't reliable).

Latency under concurrent load — the operating reality

The idle number isn't the operating number. On one shared connection, as bulk telemetry ramps 0→150 KB/s the stop-signal's p99 round-trip climbs 19→60 ms and the fraction over 30 ms goes 0→99.9% — yet nothing is lost (zero timeouts, zero disconnects, every run): it queues behind the bulk. That's unpaced — telemetry fired as fast as the target rate allows, so writes pile up in the radio's send queue and the stop-signal waits behind them. Paced — the sender waits for each bulk write to finish before issuing the next (one write outstanding), keeping the queue shallow — holds p99 flat at ~26–28 ms and >30 ms under 0.5% at the same load. It's queue depth, not throughput, that sets the tail — a strong single-connection mitigation, though not a hard bound. 7.5 ms interval, 2M, n=2, per-stage percentiles (p99 = the slowest 1% of round-trips; p99.9 = slowest 0.1%).

Method: receiver-delivered goodput, steady-window least-squares; one run per cell except the open-CoC 50/75/100 ms tail (n=2 deep-buffer re-runs). Hardware: 2× Nordic nRF54L15, 2M PHY (the 2 Msym/s physical layer). Stacks: open = Zephyr ll_sw_split (its open-source link-layer controller); proprietary = Nordic SoftDevice Controller (SDC), built with nRF Connect SDK (NCS) v3.4.0. The primary sweep is one session; the CoC tail, the interval-latency panel, and the latency-under-load panel (n=2 per-stage percentiles, 2026-08-14) are later/separate passes. CoC-duplex is omitted (n=1, under investigation). Provenance: derived per-cell CSVs, run scripts, and firmware sources are archived; raw endpoint logs, resolved configs, and flashed binaries/hashes are not — treat as indicative, not independently re-runnable as shown. 1 KB = 1024 B.

Prior art — established, re-measured or relied on

For contrast with what's new (top of this section): most of what these curves show is established BLE behavior we re-measured for this hardware, with citations.

  • BLE 2M throughput ceilings (~150–185 KB/s) and that throughput peaks mid-interval — known airtime physics.
  • FSU / Shorter Connection Intervals as spec features — Bluetooth SIG Core 6.0. The open-stack controller implementation is unmerged upstream WIP — PR #82324 (kruithofa, closed/DNM) → PR #99473 (cvinayak, open); only the host side #93783 merged. We imported and defect-fixed that base.
  • Latency ≈ the connection interval, and head-of-line blocking under load — textbook BLE / networking.
  • GATT vs. L2CAP CoC transports — long-standing.

FAQ — for a teleop / remote-control robotics evaluator

What this does and doesn't tell you

Honest answers to the questions this data raises. Each is tagged answered (measured), partial (first data, not a full result), or roadmap (not yet measured). The throughput/latency charts above are a clean-bench characterization, not a safety qualification — the tags say where that line falls.

When BLE drops entirely, does the robot fail safe? answered

Yes, by design, and the safety action is decoupled from the link. An independent actuator-side watchdog holds a GPIO STOP that de-asserts if the heartbeat stops (dead-man). First hardware test: ~200 ms heartbeat-loss→STOP, microsecond-scale watchdog-expiry→STOP, and a power-loss fail-safe passed (n=2, first measurement). So if the radio fails, the actuator stops regardless — the guarantee doesn't depend on the link staying up. Not yet a full safety qualification.

What's the guaranteed worst-case stop latency? partial

Over the BLE link alone: not hard-bounded — p99 ~28 ms with completion-pacing, rare tens-of-ms tail; a hard bound would need a separate connection or link-layer priority (not measured). The hard bound comes from the watchdog above, not the link. Intended architecture: soft-real-time stop-signal over BLE for normal operation + the independent watchdog for the guarantee.

Does it stay connected over hours/days? answered (clean RF)

Yes, under a day of continuous cycling load. A 25 h soak of the paced config drove 4.9M round-trips across 92 idle→saturated load cycles with 0 disconnects, 0 losses, 0 faults, 100% observed availability. First-pass fault-injection also survived board resets + a power-cycle (0 fatal, 3–9 s reconnect). Still roadmap: the same reliability under degraded RF, ≥25× reset endurance, and multi-day rare-event bounds. Clean-RF continuous uptime is established; the throughput/latency curves themselves are not the reliability claim — this soak is.

What happens at range / through walls / with interference? roadmap

Not yet measured — the biggest open question for a real deployment. Every number here is bench-clean (boards adjacent, quiet RF). Expect throughput and latency to degrade and loss to rise with range/obstruction/contention; the queue-depth/pacing behavior should still hold. A degraded-RF campaign is planned.

Does it interoperate with a phone/tablet/gateway, or need your chip on both ends? answered

The throughput and latency shown here use standard spec timing (7.5 ms+) and standard GATT/CoC — so they interoperate with any spec-compliant BLE device. (A separate sub-millisecond latency result, not on this page, is non-spec and requires our controller on both ends — a two-device link only.)

Is ~170–185 KB/s enough for my telemetry? partial

~170 KB/s ≈ 1.4 Mbit/s — a control-and-telemetry budget, not video. Comfortable for a heartbeat, a stop-signal, and moderate sensor/pose/status streams concurrently; not camera feeds. Map it to your payload (rate × bytes) to confirm.

Which number should I design to? answered

A conservative floor, not the peak. Same-day RF varied ~11–18%, so treat ~150 KB/s as a safe sustained floor and 15–25 ms as the interval sweet spot; ~185 is best-case. Latency: budget for the under-load tail, not the idle number.

Why BLE, vs Wi-Fi Direct / proprietary 2.4 GHz / UWB? answered

For a failover safety link (not the primary high-bandwidth path), BLE wins on ubiquity, power, cost, and an open auditable stack. On latency it's competitive: our ~915 µs research floor sits near proprietary 2.4 GHz (ESB ~600 µs), UWB (~1 ms), and 5G URLLC (1 ms); wired EtherCAT (250 µs) is lower. The alternatives buy lower latency at higher cost/power/complexity.

What's turnkey vs. research I'd have to fund? answered

Turnkey-ish: the 7.5 ms spec-timing link, completion-pacing (app-level, straightforward), and the watchdog fail-safe pattern. Research/fund: reliable sub-ms (non-spec, reliability unsolved), degraded-RF qualification, fleet scaling, and any safety certification.

Is nRF54L15 the chip I'd ship? Does this generalize? partial

nRF54L15 is Nordic's current BLE 6 part; we also have nRF52 data (~150 KB/s, ~5% lower). The open Zephyr stack is vendor-portable in principle, but other silicon isn't measured here — numbers are nRF54L15-specific, the architecture generalizes.

One relay, many robots — does it scale? roadmap

Only a single pair is measured; multi-connection/fleet scaling is not, and the fast experimental firmware is single-connection. Roadmap.

Power draw? Certification? roadmap

Power: not characterized — a saturated 15 ms link is not low-power (that's the throughput-ceiling regime); a heartbeat + stop-signal at a relaxed interval is. Depends on duty cycle. Certification: out of scope — a safety-rated link may need functional-safety (e.g. IEC 61508 / ISO 13849) and RF regulatory work; the open stack aids auditability but is not itself a certification.
